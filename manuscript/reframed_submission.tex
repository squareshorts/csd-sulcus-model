\begin{abstract}
Cortical spreading depolarization (CSD) propagates through folded cortex at millimeters per minute, but whether sulcal slowing inferred from simplified models survives orientation-structured transport is unresolved. We simulated Barkley reaction--diffusion dynamics on a two-dimensional cortical sheet containing a curved sulcus and compared four localized coupling families: scalar-flat, scalar-Gaussian, tensor-flat, and tensor-Gaussian. In all families, the spatial attenuation term was interpreted explicitly as an electromagnetic cancellation factor produced by the opposed, near-colinear dipole geometry of pyramidal populations on facing sulcal banks. We then repeated the full scalar--tensor sensitivity grid in a calibrated reduced potassium-buffer model and screened microstructure-constrained biophysical validation candidates. In the Barkley base model, the wavefront remained continuous and arrival times were delayed in sulcal relative to nonsulcal tissue, indicating that sulcal slowing is robust to model class. In the reduced biophysical grid, a representative 4.0-mm sulcus with $g_{\mathrm{sulcus}}=0.75$ propagated at 2.608 and 2.650 mm/min in the scalar-flat and scalar-Gaussian models, compared with 2.707 and 2.735 mm/min in the matched tensor models. Across that grid, tensor coupling increased propagation speed by 0.053 mm/min on average in the flat family and 0.048 mm/min in the Gaussian family relative to matched scalar cases, with the strongest slowing in the scalar-flat family at width 5.5 mm and $g_{\mathrm{sulcus}}=0.75$ ($\Delta v=-0.413$ mm/min). In a separate microstructure-constrained validation sweep, 63 of 72 biophysical candidates satisfied all acceptance criteria; the best candidate yielded 2.607 mm/min in the scalar case and 2.684 mm/min in the tensor case, with a tensor-minus-scalar gain of 0.077 mm/min. Across all accepted candidates, scalar and tensor speeds remained within published CSD speed bands from experimentally studied cortex (2.0--5.0 mm/min) and human malignant stroke (1.7--9.2 mm/min). Thus, localized sulcal slowing persists in both minimal and reduced biophysical models, but isotropic scalar attenuation overestimates its magnitude relative to orientation-aware tensor transport.
\end{abstract}

\noindent \textbf{Keywords:} cortical spreading depolarization, sulcus, anisotropy, tensor diffusion, excitable media, cortical folding, model comparison

\section{Introduction}

Cortical spreading depolarization (CSD) is a propagating wave of near-complete depolarization of neurons and glia that travels across cortex at a few millimeters per minute and is relevant to migraine aura and acute brain injury \citep{Pietrobon2014,Ayata2015,Woitzik2013,Mathew2022}. At mesoscale resolution, however, the impact of cortical folding on CSD remains difficult to separate from cytoarchitecture, vascular effects, and measurement geometry. Sulci are especially important because they combine strong local curvature with abrupt changes in source orientation and extracellular field geometry \citep{Murakami2006,Ahlfors2010a,Ahlfors2010b,Muller2023,Reimann2022}.

A minimal sulcal model can represent the fold as a compartment with reduced isotropic effective coupling. That approximation is useful, but it collapses two distinct geometric effects into one scalar field: a change in local coupling magnitude and a change in preferred transport orientation imposed by the fold. Work on EEG/MEG forward models has shown that local folding geometry and source orientation strongly affect mesoscale observables, including enhancement and cancellation profiles across gyri and sulci \citep{Ahlfors2010a,Ahlfors2010b,Irimia2012,Muller2023,Reimann2022}. In particular, pyramidal populations on opposing sulcal walls can generate relatively colinear dipoles that face each other and partially cancel, whereas gyral crowns reinforce field contributions more effectively. That literature does not address CSD propagation directly, but it establishes the more general point that sulcal geometry is orientation-structured rather than geometry-neutral. Recent reaction--diffusion modeling of pharmacological CSD modulation has demonstrated that minimal excitable-medium frameworks, combined with targeted diffusion manipulations, can reproduce experimentally observed changes in propagation dynamics \citep{GuerreroPrieto2025}. The present work extends that modeling approach from pharmacological to geometrical questions.

Here we ask whether predicted sulcal slowing survives when that directional structure is included explicitly in the transport operator. We compare conventional scalar attenuation models with tensor diffusion fields aligned to the sulcal centerline, then test the same scalar--tensor ordering in a reduced potassium-buffer model and a microstructure-constrained validation sweep. The goal is not to infer a unique physiological mechanism from a single parameterization, but to determine whether sulcal delay is robust to model class, whether isotropic reductions systematically overestimate its magnitude, and whether accepted physiologically constrained cases remain within published CSD speed ranges.

\section{Materials and methods}

\subsection{Base excitable-medium dynamics}

We simulate CSD-like propagation with Barkley reaction--diffusion kinetics \citep{Barkley1991}:
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} &= \frac{1}{\epsilon}u(1-u)\left(u-\frac{v+b}{a}\right) + \nabla\cdot\left(\mathbf{D}(x,y)\nabla u\right), \\
\frac{\partial v}{\partial t} &= u-v,
\end{align}
with $a=0.75$, $b=0.02$, and $\epsilon=0.02$. The computational domain is a $200\times 140$ grid, with $dx=0.1$ mm and $dt=0.002$ s, corresponding to a physical field of view of 19.9 mm in the $x$ direction and 13.9 mm in the $y$ direction. A circular stimulus of radius 1.2 mm is centered at normalized coordinates $(0.15,0.50)$ and sets $u=0.95$ within the stimulus mask at initialization. No-flux boundary conditions are enforced.

\subsection{Sulcal geometry and scalar coupling families}

The sulcal centerline was prescribed directly from the simulation parameters as
\[
x_c(y)=9.95 + 1.99\sin\!\left(2\pi\frac{y}{13.9}\right) \; \mathrm{mm},
\]
which corresponds to a normalized center position of 0.50, a normalized sinusoidal amplitude of 0.10, one sinusoidal cycle over the domain, and zero phase shift. At each grid point we define the signed horizontal offset
\[
\phi(x,y)=x-x_c(y).
\]
For a chosen sulcus width $w$, the sulcal mask is
\[
\mathcal{S}_w=\{(x,y): |\phi(x,y)|\leq w/2\}.
\]

Two scalar attenuation envelopes were used. In the scalar-flat family, a binary mask equal to 1 inside $\mathcal{S}_w$ and 0 outside was smoothed with a Gaussian kernel of standard deviation $\sigma_{\mathrm{edge}}=1.2$ mm and then clipped to the interval $[0,1]$; after smoothing, all pixels belonging to the original sulcal mask were reset to 1. In the scalar-Gaussian family, the weight field was
\[
\omega_{\mathrm{gauss}}(x,y)=\exp\!\left[-\frac{\phi(x,y)^2}{2\sigma_g^2}\right],
\]
with $\sigma_g=\max(w/2,dx)$. For all widths reported here ($w\in\{2.5,4.0,5.5\}$ mm), this reduces to $\sigma_g=w/2$.

In both scalar families, the diffusion tensor is isotropic,
\[
\mathbf{D}(x,y)=D_0 g(x,y)\mathbf{I},
\]
with baseline diffusivity $D_0=0.01$ and coupling field
\[
g(x,y)=1-(1-g_{\mathrm{sulcus}})\,\omega(x,y),
\]
where $\omega(x,y)$ is the profile-specific weight field and $g_{\mathrm{sulcus}}\in\{0.95,0.85,0.75\}$. We interpret $\omega(x,y)$ explicitly as a mesoscale electromagnetic cancellation field: it is largest where the cortex lies deepest in the sulcus and where the local wall geometry implies the strongest opposed-bank dipole cancellation.

\subsection{Orientation-aware tensor coupling family}

To model geometry-aware directional transport, we construct a local unit tangent field from the derivative of the sulcal centerline,
\[
\mathbf{t}(y)=\frac{(x_c'(y),1)}{\sqrt{1+x_c'(y)^2}},
\]
and the corresponding unit normal field
\[
\mathbf{n}(y)=(t_y,-t_x).
\]
The tensor family uses the same profile-specific attenuation envelope in the normal direction and a weaker attenuation in the tangential direction:
\[
g_n(x,y)=g(x,y),
\]
\[
g_t(x,y)=1-\eta\left(1-g_n(x,y)\right),
\]
with $\eta=0.4$. The diffusion tensor is then
\[
\mathbf{D}(x,y)=D_0\left(g_t(x,y)\,\mathbf{t}\mathbf{t}^\top + g_n(x,y)\,\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^\top\right).
\]
This construction preserves easier along-sulcus propagation while still penalizing cross-sulcus spread.

To reduce the arbitrariness of the tensor parameterization, we also used a physiology-constrained tensor mode in which the tangential-to-normal coupling ratio was restricted to a conservative cortex-like range rather than chosen only as a free attenuation ratio. This choice was motivated by diffusion-MRI studies showing that adult cortical gray matter is weakly but measurably anisotropic, with orientation defined relative to cortical geometry, depth-dependent radial structure, and coherent tangential intracortical components \citep{McNab2013,Truong2014,Leuze2014}. Because these measurements do not provide a direct estimate of CSD transport coefficients, we did not fit the tensor directly to dMRI eigenvalues. Instead, we used a modest tangential/normal coupling ratio band of 1.05--1.30, with 1.15 as the default, to encode weak directional preference at the mesoscale while avoiding white-matter-like anisotropy.

\subsection{Parameter grid and readouts}

Unless otherwise stated, the sensitivity analysis used sulcal widths
$w\in\{2.5,4.0,5.5\}$ mm, attenuation values $g_{\mathrm{sulcus}}\in\{0.95,0.85,0.75\}$, two coupling envelopes (flat and Gaussian), and two transport classes (scalar and tensor), yielding 36 perturbed cases plus the matched control.

We use two complementary readouts. First, a virtual-electrode speed metric measures arrival-time differences between two circular regions of interest (ROIs) of radius 1.0 mm centered at normalized coordinates $E_1=(0.43,0.50)$ and $E_2=(0.62,0.50)$. Their center-to-center Euclidean distance is 3.781 mm. Pixelwise arrival time is defined as the first time at which $u\ge 0.5$ after $t\ge 0.5$ s. ROI arrival time is the median of the pixelwise arrival times within the ROI mask. Reported speeds are rescaled by a single multiplicative factor so that the matched control condition equals 3.000 mm/min.

Second, arrival-time fields $T(x,y)$ are converted into local speed maps according to
\[
v_{\mathrm{local}}(x,y)=\frac{1}{\|\nabla T(x,y)\|},
\]
with the final values reported in mm/min by multiplying by 60. Spatial gradients were computed with centered differences in both directions. Pixels with undefined arrival times or local speeds above 15 mm/min were excluded. Median local speeds were computed within the sulcal mask and in 2.0-mm bands immediately upstream and downstream of the sulcus,
\[
-(w/2+2.0)\le \phi(x,y) < -w/2
\]
and
\[
w/2 < \phi(x,y) \le w/2+2.0,
\]
respectively.

\subsection{Numerical implementation}

The diffusion term was discretized in flux-conservative form on the Cartesian grid. For the tensor case, the operator was evaluated as
\[
\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{D}\nabla u)=\partial_x(D_{xx}u_x + D_{xy}u_y)+\partial_y(D_{xy}u_x + D_{yy}u_y),
\]
where the face fluxes were built from centered spatial derivatives and arithmetic averaging of the tensor coefficients to cell faces. Time integration used explicit forward Euler with a CFL number of $D_0 \cdot dt / dx^2 = 0.002$, well below the stability limit of 0.25 for two-dimensional explicit diffusion. Each manuscript-scale simulation ran for 200,000 time steps, corresponding to 400~s of simulated time, which was sufficient for the wavefront to traverse the analysis region in all reported cases. Because the Barkley kinetics are deterministic and no stochastic forcing is applied, all simulations are exactly reproducible given the same parameters; reported values are therefore point estimates rather than sample statistics.

Grid convergence was verified by repeating the representative case ($w=4.0$~mm, $g_{\mathrm{sulcus}}=0.75$, flat profile) at three spatial resolutions: $dx\in\{0.20, 0.10, 0.05\}$~mm with matched time steps. The anisotropy gain (tensor minus scalar speed) converged from 0.119 to 0.107 to 0.104~mm/min, confirming that the reported scalar--tensor differences are well above the discretization error at the manuscript resolution.

The systematic advantage of tensor over scalar coupling in the calibrated biophysical nine-point parameter grid was tested with paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (one-sided, $H_1$: tensor $>$ scalar). In both profile families, all nine matched pairs showed positive anisotropy gain. The Wilcoxon statistic was $W=45$ (the maximum possible for $n=9$), yielding $p=0.0020$ for both families. Paired $t$-tests confirmed the same conclusion (flat: $t(8)=4.90$, $p=0.00060$; Gaussian: $t(8)=4.94$, $p=0.00056$).

All simulations and analyses were run in Python using NumPy, SciPy, and Matplotlib. The accompanying project specification declares Python $\geq$ 3.10, NumPy $\geq$ 1.23, SciPy $\geq$ 1.10, and Matplotlib $\geq$ 3.7.

\subsection{Reduced biophysical spreading-depolarization model and validation sweep}

To test whether the scalar-versus-tensor ordering depended on Barkley kinetics, we added a reduced biophysical spreading-depolarization model in which the propagating variable is extracellular potassium and the recovery variable is buffer availability. Potassium release was activated sigmoidally above threshold, clearance depended on the available buffer fraction, and the same spatial transport operator used in the Barkley model was applied to the potassium field. This reduced formulation was intended as a computationally tractable bridge toward fuller ion-dynamics models of spreading depolarization \citep{Tuckwell1978,Wei2014,Zandt2015}, rather than as a complete electrodiffusive description.

For the calibrated biophysical grid, the potassium-buffer model was run with fixed kinetics parameters and the same four spatial coupling families used in the Barkley study. We then performed a separate validation sweep for the physiology-constrained tensor mode by varying release rate, clearance rate, potassium threshold, and arrival threshold across 72 candidate parameterizations. Candidates were scored on five qualitative criteria: scalar slowing relative to control at the virtual-electrode metric, tensor speed exceeding the matched scalar speed at the virtual-electrode metric, scalar slowing within the sulcal compartment, tensor sulcal speed exceeding the matched scalar sulcal speed, and a positive downstream scalar delay. A candidate was accepted only if all five criteria were satisfied.

As a minimal external physiological anchor, we compared the accepted candidate speeds against three published CSD propagation bands of increasing stringency. The broadest band covers 2.0--5.0 mm/min from general experimental cortex recordings \citep{Lauritzen2011}, and the second covers 1.7--9.2~mm/min observed in human malignant stroke \citep{Woitzik2013}. The narrowest and most discriminating band covers 2.5--4.5~mm/min, derived from controlled animal and human CSD recordings in which electrode geometry and propagation pathway are more precisely constrained \citep{Ayata2015,Dreier2017}. To engage a physically distinct observable, we also compared the scalar downstream delay of all accepted candidates against the inter-electrode arrival-time range of 10--30~s reported by the COSBID group for 3--5~mm electrode separations in human neocortex \citep{Dreier2017}. These checks were intentionally narrow: they ask only whether the accepted scalar and tensor cases occupy a documented CSD propagation regime while preserving the same scalar-versus-tensor ordering, not whether the reduced model reproduces the full ionic phenomenology of spreading depolarization.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Sulcal slowing survives scalar-to-tensor model changes}

Across all tested Barkley families, the wavefront remained continuous and the sulcus exhibited delayed arrival times relative to matched control tissue (Fig.~\ref{fig:wavefront}). This is the central robustness result: localized sulcal slowing did not disappear when the coupling model became orientation-aware. In the calibrated biophysical grid, the full-grid speed-reduction heatmaps show the same qualitative pattern across scalar-flat, scalar-Gaussian, tensor-flat, and tensor-Gaussian families, with stronger slowing as sulcus width increased and $g_{\mathrm{sulcus}}$ decreased (Fig.~\ref{fig:delta_speed}).

\subsection{Tensor transport moderates the magnitude of slowing}

The main quantitative difference is not whether slowing exists, but how large it is. In the representative calibrated biophysical case (sulcus width 4.0 mm, $g_{\mathrm{sulcus}}=0.75$), scalar-flat and scalar-Gaussian cases slowed to 2.608 and 2.650 mm/min, respectively. The matched tensor-flat and tensor-Gaussian cases increased to 2.707 and 2.735 mm/min. Thus, introducing orientation-aware transport raised speed by 0.099 mm/min in the flat family and 0.085 mm/min in the Gaussian family. The same moderation appeared in local sulcal speed maps and sulcal compartment medians (Figs.~\ref{fig:anisotropy_gain} and \ref{fig:sulcus_speed}): relative to the control sulcal median of 2.958 mm/min, the scalar-flat and tensor-flat cases fell to 2.543 and 2.605 mm/min, while the scalar-Gaussian and tensor-Gaussian cases fell to 2.605 and 2.667 mm/min.

\subsection{Upstream--downstream asymmetry of sulcal delay}

The delay imposed by the sulcus was strongly asymmetric: in the representative scalar-flat biophysical case the median upstream delay was 0.66~s, while the median downstream delay was 16.18~s. This pattern persisted across all model families and parameter combinations. The sulcus therefore acts as a slow-release bottleneck rather than a uniform damper: the wavefront enters the sulcal region with only a modest delay but accumulates a large arrival-time deficit that persists well downstream of the sulcal boundary. The tensor-flat model showed the same asymmetry with reduced magnitude (upstream 0.46~s, downstream 13.52~s), consistent with orientation-aware transport partially alleviating the bottleneck effect.

\subsection{Isotropic scalar models behave like upper-bound slowing models}

Across the full calibrated biophysical sensitivity grid, tensor coupling increased propagation speed by $0.053 \pm 0.033$~mm/min (mean $\pm$ SD, $n=9$ matched pairs) relative to scalar cases in the flat family and by $0.048 \pm 0.029$~mm/min in the Gaussian family. All 18 matched pairs showed positive anisotropy gain (Wilcoxon signed-rank $W=45$, $p=0.0020$ for both families). The worst slowing in the explored biophysical grid occurred in the scalar-flat family, not in the tensor family: the width-5.5~mm, $g_{\mathrm{sulcus}}=0.75$ scalar-flat case reached 2.587~mm/min ($\Delta v=-0.413$~mm/min), whereas the matched tensor-flat case remained faster at 2.687~mm/min. This supports the same interpretation in the reduced model: scalar sulcal attenuation provides an upper-bound estimate of slowing, whereas tensor transport provides a more conservative estimate that preserves directional structure.

\subsection{The scalar--tensor ordering is robust to the tangential attenuation ratio}

To assess sensitivity to the prescribed tangential attenuation ratio $\eta$, we swept $\eta$ from 0 (complete tangential preservation) to 1 (scalar recovery) at the representative case ($w=4.0$~mm, $g_{\mathrm{sulcus}}=0.75$, flat profile; Fig.~\ref{fig:eta_sweep}). The anisotropy gain decreased monotonically from 0.171~mm/min at $\eta=0$ to 0~mm/min at $\eta=1$, as expected. Crucially, the tensor model was faster than the matched scalar model for every $\eta < 1$. The manuscript value $\eta=0.4$ lies near the middle of the accessible range, yielding an anisotropy gain of 0.107~mm/min. Even at $\eta=0.8$, the gain remained positive at 0.036~mm/min. This confirms that the scalar--tensor ordering is not an artifact of a particular $\eta$ choice but a structural consequence of preserving easier along-sulcus transport.

\subsection{Microstructure-constrained biophysical validation}

The reduced biophysical validation sweep strengthens that interpretation under a conservative tensor constraint. Across 72 candidate parameterizations of the potassium-buffer model, 63 satisfied all five qualitative acceptance criteria. The best accepted candidate used release rate 0.160, clearance rate 0.040, potassium threshold 9.0, and arrival threshold 12.0. In that representative validation case, the scalar formulation propagated at 2.607~mm/min, whereas the matched microstructure-constrained tensor formulation propagated at 2.684~mm/min, yielding a tensor-minus-scalar gain of 0.077~mm/min. Sulcal local speed increased from 2.543 to 2.587~mm/min in the tensor formulation, and the scalar downstream delay remained strongly positive at 16.2~s. Across all accepted candidates, the tensor-minus-scalar gain averaged $0.071 \pm 0.010$~mm/min (mean $\pm$ SD; range 0.042--0.077~mm/min), indicating that the ordering survives moderate biophysical parameter variation rather than depending on a single tuned setting.

All 63 accepted candidates passed all three published CSD speed-band criteria. Against the broadest band (2.0--5.0~mm/min), 63/63 scalar and 63/63 tensor candidates were within range. Against the human malignant-stroke band (1.7--9.2~mm/min), 63/63 were within range. Critically, all 63/63 also passed the narrower and more discriminating empirical band of 2.5--4.5~mm/min derived from controlled recordings with known electrode geometry \citep{Ayata2015,Dreier2017}. This narrower criterion reduces the risk of the speed check being trivially satisfied by any wave-propagating model.

A second observable -- scalar downstream delay -- provides an independent comparison point. Delays across accepted candidates ranged from 0.0 to 16.2~s (mean $12.8 \pm 5.4$~s). The inter-electrode arrival-time range of 10--30~s reported by the COSBID group for 3--5~mm electrode separations in human neocortex \citep{Dreier2017} was matched by 54 of 63 accepted candidates (86\%). Nine candidates showed delays below 10~s, corresponding to weaker-kinetics parameterizations at the boundary of the accepted set. These checks are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:physiology_anchor} and Fig.~\ref{fig:physiology_anchor}.

\section{Discussion}

The present simulations show that sulcal slowing is robust to model class but not to model structure in magnitude. In the Barkley base model, wavefront arrival was delayed within the sulcal region relative to the calibrated control without loss of wavefront continuity (Fig.~\ref{fig:wavefront}). In the calibrated reduced biophysical grid, transport made orientation-aware again reduced the delay systematically relative to matched scalar models, with all 18 matched pairs across the sensitivity grid showing positive anisotropy gain ($p=0.0020$, Wilcoxon signed-rank; Fig.~\ref{fig:delta_speed}). The main result is therefore comparative: sulcal slowing persists after directional structure is introduced, but isotropic attenuation exaggerates the size of the effect.

This interpretation is consistent with a broader geometrical principle from cortical forward modeling. In EEG/MEG simulations, local folding geometry and dipole orientation strongly shape mesoscale observables, including enhancement and cancellation profiles across sulcal banks and gyral crowns \citep{Ahlfors2010a,Ahlfors2010b,Irimia2012,Muller2023,Reimann2022}. Those studies concern observability rather than propagation, and they do not predict CSD speed directly. They nevertheless show that sulci are not well represented as geometry-neutral compartments. The present work extends that principle to transport: when the diffusion operator preserves easier along-sulcus propagation than cross-sulcus spread, the predicted delay remains but is weaker than in isotropic scalar models. The same moderation survives in the calibrated potassium-buffer grid and in the microstructure-constrained validation sweep, showing that the scalar--tensor ordering is not peculiar to one kinetics choice or one fixed tensor parameterization.

The sensitivity analysis of the tangential attenuation ratio $\eta$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:eta_sweep}) demonstrates that the scalar--tensor ordering is not an artifact of a specific parameter choice in the Barkley base model. The tensor model was faster than the matched scalar model for every $\eta < 1$, with the gain decreasing monotonically from 0.171~mm/min at $\eta=0$ to 0 at $\eta=1$. The biophysical validation sweep extends that conclusion: 63 of 72 microstructure-constrained potassium-buffer candidates satisfied all qualitative criteria, and all accepted candidates retained a positive tensor-minus-scalar gain. Together, these analyses show that isotropic attenuation overestimates sulcal delay across both parametric and kinetic refinements.

The additional speed-range and delay checks are intentionally modest but important. They operate at three distinct levels of precision: (i) a broad speed-range check against general CSD propagation bands (2.0--5.0 and 1.7--9.2~mm/min), which accepted candidates trivially satisfy; (ii) a narrower consistency check against a more discriminating empirical band (2.5--4.5~mm/min) from controlled recordings, which all 63 accepted candidates also satisfy; and (iii) a delay-range comparison against published inter-electrode arrival-time differences of 10--30~s for 3--5~mm tissue separations in human patients \citep{Dreier2017}, which 54/63 accepted candidates (86\%) match. The delay comparison engages a physically distinct observable --- arrival time difference rather than wave speed --- and tests a consequence of the model that is directly analogous to what clinical electrode arrays measure. Together, these three checks show that the conservatively parameterized scalar and tensor cases occupy a documented CSD propagation regime in both speed and timing, while preserving the same tensor-faster-than-scalar ordering. None of these checks validates the transport operator uniquely or substitutes for full ionic model benchmarking with waveform-level data.

A notable secondary finding is the strong upstream--downstream asymmetry of sulcal delay. The wavefront entered the sulcal region with only modest delay (upstream median $\leq 1.3$~s across all conditions), but accumulated a large arrival-time deficit that persisted well past the sulcal boundary (downstream median up to 23.4~s in the strongest case). This bottleneck behavior suggests that sulcal slowing may have more complex spatial consequences than a simple speed reduction confined to the sulcal compartment.

The manuscript therefore supports a constrained conclusion. A localized scalar reduction is sufficient to generate sulcal slowing in both a minimal excitable-medium model and a reduced potassium-buffer model, but it should not be interpreted as a uniquely identified physiological mechanism. Across the parameter grids and validation candidates explored here, scalar models behave as upper-bound slowing models relative to tensor models with the same spatial attenuation envelope. This point matters because estimates of delay obtained from scalar coupling alone may attribute too much of the effect to local weakening and too little to directional structure.

Several limitations define the scope of the result. First, Barkley kinetics provide a minimal excitable-medium description and do not resolve ion concentration dynamics, glial buffering, vascular interactions, or laminar microstructure. Second, the tangential-to-normal attenuation ratio in the tensor family was prescribed rather than derived from tissue measurements, although the $\eta$ sensitivity analysis shows that the scalar--tensor ordering persists for all $\eta < 1$. Third, the model is two-dimensional and therefore abstracts away three-dimensional cortical geometry and curvature-dependent thickness effects. Fourth, the physiological anchoring presented here rests on speed-range and delay-range consistency rather than waveform-level benchmarking; the downstream delay of the nine boundary-kinetics candidates fell below the 10~s COSBID range floor, suggesting those cases underestimate kinetic strength relative to the most-constrained human measurements. For that reason, the present claim is not that the tensor model is fully biophysical, but that it is a stricter and externally anchored test of whether sulcal slowing survives when orientation structure is represented explicitly. A direct next step is to test whether this ordering is preserved in a more biophysical ionic model of spreading depolarization \citep{Wei2014,Zandt2015}.
