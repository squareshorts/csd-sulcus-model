predicts localized sulcal slowing of cortical spreading
depolarization in an excitable-medium model

Abstract
Cortical spreading depolarization (CSD) is a slow reaction–diffusion-like wave that
propagates through the cortex and is implicated in migraine aura and acute brain in-
jury. However, how gyral–sulcal geometry modulates CSD conduction remains incom-
pletely characterized. Here, we test a minimal, experimentally motivated hypothesis:
that reduced extracellular field strength in sulcal banks due to dipole attenuation can be
represented at the mesoscale as a local reduction ineffective spatial coupling(modeled
as a reduced diffusion coefficient) within the sulcus. We simulate an excitable-medium
reaction–diffusion system (Barkley kinetics) on a two-dimensional cortical sheet con-
taining a curved sulcus and quantify propagation using arrival-time maps and a virtual-
electrode velocity metric. Calibrating the control condition to a canonical gyral speed
of 3.00 mm/min, reducing the sulcal coupling tog sulcus = 0.75 decreases conduction
to 2.579 mm/min (∆v=−0.4208 mm/min,−14.03%). A parameter sweep shows a
smooth, monotonic dependence of velocity ong sulcus, with magnitudes consistent with

1 Introduction
Cortical spreading depolarization (CSD) is a propagating wave of near-complete depolariza-
tion of neurons and glia, accompanied by a transient depression of ongoing activity (Mathew
and Panonnummal, 2022). In migraine aura and in acute brain injury, CSD-like events
propagate slowly across the cortex, typically on the order of a few millimeters per minute
(Pietrobon and Moskowitz, 2014; Ayata and Lauritzen, 2015; Woitzik et al., 2013). While
the cellular and ionic mechanisms of CSD have been extensively studied, it remains less
clear how cortical folding geometry shapes CSD propagation at mesoscopic scales relevant
to electrocorticography, optical imaging, and clinical monitoring.
A consistent observation in MEG/EEG forward modeling is that extracellular fields
and measured signal amplitudes depend strongly on cortical folding and source orientation
(Ahlfors et al., 2010a,b). In sulcal banks, pyramidal-cell orientations can produce partial
dipole cancellation, attenuating measurable fields relative to gyral crowns (Murakami and
Okada, 2006; Ahlfors et al., 2010a; Muller et al., 2023; Reimann and Anastassiou, 2022).
Although amplitude attenuation is not itself a measure of propagation speed, it motivates
the broader hypothesis that sulcal geometry reduces effective long-range extracellular cou-
pling. Such coupling contributes to the spatial spread of depolarization and ionic gradients
during CSD, suggesting that sulcal folding may influence conduction indirectly via geometry-
2
dependent field attenuation.
Prior computational and theoretical studies have shown that cortical geometry can influ-
ence spreading-depression dynamics through curvature, anisotropy, and spatial heterogene-
ity of coupling. Reaction–diffusion models on curved or folded substrates demonstrate that
Gaussian curvature and geometric constraints can modulate wave speed, directionality, and
stability (Tuckwell and Miura, 1978; Tuckwell, 2008; Chapuisat, 2007; Kenny et al., 2019).
Patient-specific simulations further suggest that individual folding patterns and diffusivity
tensors may shape clinically observed propagation profiles (Kroos et al., 2019). However,
these approaches typically emphasize geometric curvature or anisotropic diffusion rather than
explicitly linking sulcal cytoarchitecture and dipole attenuation to a localized reduction in
effective coupling.
To test this, we use a deliberately minimal excitable-medium model with a spatially
varying coupling coefficient. We interpret the coupling reduction as a coarse-grained rep-
resentation of sulcal dipole attenuation and related extracellular-field effects: a mesoscale
parameterization, not a claim about literal molecular diffusivity. We then quantify how this
localized coupling reduction changes arrival times and conduction velocity across a curved
sulcus.

The flux-conservative discretization ensures that spatial variations inD(x, y) alter prop-

or artificial reflections at the sulcal boundary.

a spatially confined band of delayed propagation co-localized with the sulcal compartment,
while gyral regions showed minimal change (Fig. 1).

4 Discussion
We tested whether a localized reduction in effective spatial coupling within sulcal banks,
motivated by dipole cancellation and the associated attenuation of extracellular fields in the
folded cortex, is sufficient to produce localized slowing of CSD-like waves. This framing
builds on experimental and optical-imaging evidence that CSD velocity varies across cortical
regions with differing cytoarchitecture, laminar structure, and folding geometry (Vil´ agi et al.,
2001; Fujita et al., 2016; Santos et al., 2014, 2017). In a minimal excitable-medium model,
reducing sulcal coupling tog sulcus = 0.75 produced a 14.03% decrease in conduction velocity
relative to the calibrated control. The delay was spatially confined to the sulcal compartment
and did not destabilize or fragment the wavefront.
This study does not claim that dipole cancellation has been experimentally shown to
slow CSD. What is well established is that folding and source orientation shape extracellular
fields and measured amplitudes in MEG/EEG (Murakami and Okada, 2006; Ahlfors et al.,
9
2010a; Muller et al., 2023). We use that anatomical/biophysical fact to motivate a testable
mesoscale hypothesis: if effective long-range coupling is reduced in sulcal banks, conduction
delays should occur locally. Our simulations show that this hypothesis is sufficient to generate
delays of plausible magnitude.
Folded geometry and curvature can influence spreading dynamics in other models (Kenny
et al., 2019). Our contribution is complementary: we propose a specific, measurement- and
cytoarchitecture-motivated mechanism (dipole attenuation represented as reduced effective
coupling within sulci) and show that it yields (i) spatially localized delays and (ii) a quanti-
tative speed scaling consistent withv∝
√
D.
From this perspective, geometry- and cytoarchitecture-dependent modulation of effective
coupling may also contribute to reported age-related changes in CSD propagation (Guedes
and Abadie-Guedes, 2019), given that aging is associated with substantial remodeling of
cortical thickness and folding at mesoscopic scales (Lockhart and DeCarli, 2014).
The model predicts that sulcal banks should exhibit localized delays in CSD arrival times
relative to nearby gyral crowns, even when the wavefront remains continuous. The monotonic
dependence in Table 1 further predicts that any physiological or anatomical factor that deep-
ens sulcal attenuation (effectively loweringg sulcus) should increase the delay approximately
as √gsulcus.
Notably, optical imaging and electrophysiological studies have reported regionally het-
erogeneous CSD velocities associated with cytoarchitectural transitions and sulcal anatomy,
including slower propagation in paleocortical or insular regions relative to the neocortex
(Vil´ agi et al., 2001; Fujita et al., 2016). The present model predicts that such heterogeneity
can arise even in the absence of metabolic or vascular differences, purely from geometry-
dependent effective coupling.

4.1 Limitations
We intentionally used a minimal excitable-medium model rather than a fully biophysical ionic
model (Wei et al., 2014; Zandt et al., 2015). Therefore,D(x, y) should be interpreted as an
effective coupling parameter at the mesoscale, not a microscopic diffusion constant. Future
work should test whether the same geometry-dependent slowing persists in biophysical CSD
models with explicit ion dynamics, layered cortex, anisotropy, and 3D folded surfaces.
